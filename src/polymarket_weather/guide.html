Orientation

What this project is, for someone arriving cold

Polymarket runs daily markets on the weather. A typical one asks: “Will the highest temperature in London be 24°C on July 26?” You can buy YES or NO at a price between 0 and 100 cents, and the price behaves like a probability — a bin trading at 30¢ means the crowd thinks there's roughly a 30% chance it happens. When the day ends, the market settles against a named weather station's official reading. Winners get $1 per contract; losers get nothing.

Each city-day is carved into about eleven mutually exclusive bins — 22°C, 23°C, 24°C and so on — plus separate markets for the day's lowest temperature. Exactly one bin can win.

The premise of this project was simple and, on paper, reasonable: weather is one of the few things humans forecast well and publicly. National meteorological agencies run enormous physics simulations and publish the output free. If you take those forecasts, calibrate them properly against what the stations actually recorded, and convert them into probabilities for each temperature bin, you should be able to spot bins the crowd has mispriced.

That is what was built, in roughly this shape:

Measurement

How you score a forecast — and why we stopped looking at profit

Three numbers appear throughout this report. Understanding what each one can and cannot tell you is most of the story.

Brier score — the scoreboard

You said 30%; it happened. Your error is (1 − 0.30)² = 0.49. You said 30%; it didn't happen. Your error is (0 − 0.30)² = 0.09. Average that over every bin you priced, and you have the Brier score. Lower is better; 0 is perfect.

Its virtue is that it punishes both kinds of failure at once. A forecaster who hedges everything at 50% never looks stupid but never scores well. One who says 99% and is wrong takes an enormous hit. To score well you have to be both right and appropriately confident.

CRPS — the temperature-level score

Brier scores your answer to a yes/no question. CRPS scores the whole temperature distribution you produced, in degrees. It rewards a forecast whose spread honestly reflects its uncertainty. It matters here because a model can get better at temperatures while getting worse at bin probabilities — and, as you'll see in the QRF entries, that is exactly what happened.

ROI — the one that lies

Return on investment is what everybody wants to look at, and it is the least reliable number on the page. Over 213 bets this project has watched ROI read −22.5% one day and −8.7% the next, on essentially the same book, because a handful of large positions settled overnight. ROI blends forecast quality with bet selection, sizing luck, and which markets happened to resolve — so on a few hundred bets it is mostly noise.

The rule this project runs on

Read the Brier, not the ROI. A forecaster with a worse Brier can post a positive ROI for weeks. If the model cannot out-predict the price, any profit is luck being collected on borrowed time.

What “beating the market” actually requires

It is not enough to forecast the weather well. The market price is already a good forecast — it aggregates everyone else's models, plus judgement. To profit, our probability has to be closer to the truth than the price, by enough to cover the spread crossed on entry and the fee paid on winnings. Being equally good is a losing position, because costs are real and edge is zero.

Reference points

The three things every model was measured against

The market price

Benchmark

The live Polymarket price for each bin, taken as a probability. The thing we are trying to beat.

Score

Brier 0.1158 over 261 graded markets.

Why it's hard

Its prices turn out to be almost perfectly calibrated on exactly the bins our model flagged as mispriced. Where we disagreed most strongly with the price, we were usually the ones who were wrong.

Raw ensemble

Baseline

Open-Meteo's combined ICON + GFS + ECMWF ensemble — 122 separate physics simulations of the same day, each started from slightly different initial conditions.

Mechanism

The spread between those 122 members is itself an uncertainty estimate: when the simulations agree, the atmosphere is predictable; when they scatter, it isn't. Fit a Student-t distribution to the member spread and you have bin probabilities, with no learned parameters at all.

Score

Brier 0.1360 · CRPS 1.0753 °C.

Significance

This is the number that matters most in the entire report. It is what every model in Family A was built to improve on — and, four months in, nothing has. A calibration layer that cannot beat the raw physics it is calibrating is doing damage, not work.

NWP fallback

Safety net

A deliberately dumb predictor: the deterministic forecast plus a fixed standard-deviation table by city and lead time.

Role

Used only when ensemble data is missing, so a feed outage degrades the forecast rather than dropping the city. Never a contender; included here because it defines the floor.

Family A

Calibration — teaching the physics forecast to know its own error

The central idea of this family: raw weather models are biased and overconfident in known, learnable ways. A given station may run half a degree warm; a 3-day forecast is less certain than the ensemble spread suggests. Learn those corrections from history, apply them, and you get sharper, better-calibrated probabilities than the raw model. This is textbook, respected meteorology. It failed here five separate times.

A1 · EMOS v1

retired 2026-07 Failed — trained on the wrong error

Ensemble Model Output Statistics: a regression that maps the ensemble forecast onto the observed temperature, learning both a bias correction and an honest spread.

Mechanism

Fit mean = a + b × forecast against history, and separately learn how wrong that mean typically is, so the width of the predictive distribution reflects measured error rather than the ensemble's own optimism.

The flaw

It was trained against ERA5 reanalysis — a scientific reconstruction of past weather built after the fact, using observations that were not available when a real forecast had to be made. Reanalysis is superb for climate research and completely wrong for this: it made the forecasting problem look far easier than it is.

Result

The model understated live forecast error by 2–3× at the lead times we actually bet on. That produced exactly the pathology you would predict: distributions far too narrow, extreme confidence on bins, and heavy losses when the real atmosphere landed in a tail the model had priced at near-zero.

Taught us

Train on what you will have at decision time, not on the best reconstruction available later. This is the same failure as backtesting a trading strategy on revised economic data. The fix drove the entire design of its successor.

A2 · EMOS v2

current default Ships — still loses to both baselines

The rebuilt calibrator, trained on real archived forecasts: the actual predictions the agencies published on the day, recovered from Open-Meteo's Previous Runs archive back to 2022.

Mechanism

A separate model is fitted for each lead time from 1 to 7 days, because a 1-day forecast and a 5-day forecast are different problems with different error sizes. Per lead: mean = a + b×input + seasonal term, and sigma = max(ensemble spread + diurnal boost, measured residual error), with the tail heaviness (Student-t nu) estimated from how often reality lands far out.

Two safeguards worth naming. The mean correction is self-gated: at each lead it must beat the raw input on held-out data, or the raw input is served instead. The sigma floor is never gated — it is the direct fix for v1's overconfidence, so it is not allowed to be optimised away.

Training uses the earliest 80% of dates and validates on the most recent 20%, so the coefficients in service today were fitted on data ending around August 2025 — comfortably before the markets being scored.

Result

Brier 0.1417 versus the market's 0.1158 and the raw ensemble's 0.1360. CRPS 1.0931 versus the ensemble's 1.0753.

It loses to the market, and it loses to the raw physics it is built on top of.

Diagnosis

The failure is in the tails, not the centre. Calibration made the distribution sharper, and sharpness is only a virtue when you are right. Bins the model priced in the 0–10% band came in at 15.5% — it kept ruling out outcomes that then happened, and each of those is expensive under Brier.

Taught us

Fixing the training data fixed the diagnosis but not the disease. A calibration layer can only redistribute the information already in the forecast; if the market has more information, no amount of correcting the physics closes that gap.

A3 · Random Forest calibrator

removed Failed — net negative

Replace the linear calibration with a machine-learning model that could learn arbitrary non-linear corrections from the ensemble features.

Rationale

The relationship between ensemble output and observed temperature might not be linear — perhaps the correction should differ in hot versus cold regimes, or by season, in ways a straight line cannot express.

Result

Verified net-negative on held-out data and deleted. More flexibility bought more overfitting, not more accuracy.

Taught us

The first appearance of a pattern that recurs throughout this catalogue: added model complexity did not help. The constraint was never the flexibility of the functional form.

A4 · Quantile Regression Forest — v1

2026-07-24 Failed gate M1 — 0.1713 vs 0.1685

A fully non-parametric forecaster. Rather than assuming the temperature follows a bell-shaped curve and estimating its parameters, learn the shape of the distribution directly from data.

Rationale

Every model to this point assumed a Student-t distribution and argued about its width. But the diagnosed failure was distributional — over-sharp, thin tails. If the assumed shape is wrong, tuning its parameters cannot save it. A Quantile Regression Forest (Meinshausen, 2006) makes no shape assumption at all: it grows a random forest, then reads the distribution of training outcomes that land in the same leaves as the current case.

The design flaw

To fit the existing pipeline, the learned distribution was moment-matched back to a Student-t: compute the QRF's mean, spread and kurtosis, then serve the Student-t with those properties. In hindsight this discarded the only thing that made the approach interesting — a non-parametric shape was collapsed back into the parametric one it was meant to replace.

Result

QRF 0.1713 versus ensemble 0.1685. Failed. A follow-up diagnostic confirmed the collapse was lossy in all five cities: the raw QRF quantiles scored 3–11% better on CRPS than their own Student-t approximation.

Taught us

The self-gate was scoring the wrong object. Two cities were rejected for being bad when it was the collapse, not the forest, that was bad. Measure the thing you actually serve.

A5 · Quantile Regression Forest — v2 (empirical)

2026-07-25 Failed gate M1 — 0.1820 vs 0.1767

The same forest, with the collapse removed: serve the learned distribution directly.

Mechanism

A semi-parametric predictive distribution — 99 learned quantiles forming the body, with Gaussian tails grafted beyond the outermost knots so probabilities stay well-defined at extremes. This required threading an optional CDF function through the probability machinery and the pricing engine, and letting QRF stand alone rather than being averaged with the ensemble (averaging would have re-thinned the very tails the fix widened).

Result

The fix worked on its own terms. Per-city holdout CRPS, QRF versus ensemble:

Taught us

The single most valuable finding in the catalogue: better temperature forecasts do not automatically produce better bin probabilities. CRPS improved; Brier got worse. Winning by a tenth of a degree in the middle of the distribution is worth little when the question is which one-degree bucket the reading lands in — and the market is already excellent at that.

QRF is now parked: merged, evaluation-only, self-gated off. It never touches production.

Family B

Better inputs — if calibration can't help, feed it a better forecast

Different meteorological agencies run different physics on different grids. They fail in different ways, and errors that are independent partly cancel when averaged. This family is about sourcing: which forecasts go into the model in the first place.

B1 · Multi-model blend

adopted Works — best available input

Average several agencies' deterministic forecasts, with the membership of that average chosen per city by held-out accuracy.

Mechanism

Candidate models are scored per city and per lead time on paired common windows — the same dates for every candidate, since the archives start at different times and an unfair window would flatter whichever model has the easier history.

Canada's GEM earned a place almost everywhere. Météo-France helped in the Atlantic cities. ECMWF's AIFS — a machine-learning weather model rather than a physics one — gave Seoul 5–8% at every lead. Japan's JMA helped Seoul and nowhere else, which is geographically sensible. China's CMA and Australia's BOM were tested and rejected as neutral or harmful.

Result

Genuinely the best input available, and all five cities now select it. But a better input fed into a losing model produces a slightly less losing model — the blend improves the forecast without closing the gap to the price.

Operational note

The blend is strictly all-or-nothing at serve time: if any member model is missing from the live feed, the whole blend is skipped rather than silently computed from a different mix of models than it was trained on. A deliberate anti-skew rule that costs coverage to protect correctness.

B2 · NBM — the US National Blend of Models

tested, not selected Lost to our own blend

The US National Weather Service's official blended guidance, produced for the exact airport stations two of our markets settle on.

Rationale

Our worst cities are the American ones. NBM is professional, station-specific guidance — tuned by meteorologists for precisely these locations. It ought to have been better than anything we assembled ourselves.

Result

It lost, and not narrowly. At Chicago O'Hare, lead-1 error of 1.94 °C versus our blend's 1.19 °C. Similar at LaGuardia. Not selected.

Taught us

A genuinely useful negative result. It rules out the obvious explanation for the US deficit: the American cities are not hard because our forecasts there are bad. Professional station guidance did worse. Whatever makes Chicago and New York difficult — more sophisticated market participants, more traded volume, sharper prices — is not something a better temperature forecast fixes.

Family C

Conditioning — using information the forecast doesn't contain

A forecast issued at midnight is a prediction. By 2pm, part of that day has already happened, and the highest temperature so far is a fact. This family tried to exploit information that arrives after the forecast is made — the most promising idea in the project, and the one whose failure was most instructive.

C1 · Intraday conditioning

built, live Premise falsified

On the target day itself, condition the forecast on hourly station observations as they arrive.

Mechanism

Two mechanisms, both sound. First, a hard floor: if the station has already recorded 24°C, the day's maximum cannot end below 24°C, so all probability below that is removed and redistributed. Second, a per-city, per-hour regression Tmax = a + b×forecast + c×(running max), self-gated hour by hour.

It works exactly as physics says it should. By 2pm local the predictive spread roughly halves. By 5pm it is around 0.4°C, with the running maximum carrying almost all the weight (c ≈ 0.95) — by late afternoon the day has essentially already told you its answer.

The test

If this were an edge, the model should beat the market more on same-day markets once the running maximum is known. Split the same-day markets by whether that information was available:

Same-day markets, split by whether the running maximum was known
RegimenModelMarketGap
Running max known670.1650.117+0.049
Morning, not yet known1120.1360.109+0.027

The opposite of the prediction. The model does worse relative to the market precisely when it has the extra information — and the market loses none of its edge.

Taught us

The running maximum is public information. Anyone trading these markets can read the same METAR feed, and they price it at least as well as we do — faster, and with fewer intermediate steps. Sophistication cannot beat information parity against a quicker counterparty.

This killed the planned successor project (a QRF trained on intraday snapshots) before a line of it was written — a read-only analysis costing an afternoon replaced a multi-week build that the data says would have failed.

C2 · Dedicated minimum-temperature model

2026-07-04 Fixed a real defect

About a fifth of these markets settle on the day's lowest temperature. They were being priced off the maximum-temperature distribution.

The defect

The output was not slightly wrong, it was nonsense — the model priced bins at 0.2% that the market had at 38%. Using a distribution built for daily highs to price daily lows is a category error.

Fix

A parallel stack mirroring the maximum-temperature one end to end: its own per-lead calibration, its own archived training data, its own live inputs, and intraday conditioning with a ceiling rather than a floor (the overnight low can only go down, and is largely locked in by mid-morning — London's spread is about 0.7°C by noon).

Where a city has no trained minimum-temperature model, those bins are skipped entirely rather than mispriced. Silence beats confident nonsense.

Result

A correctness fix, not an edge. It stopped the model producing garbage on a fifth of the market universe; it did not make that fifth profitable.

Family D

Distribution shape — the diagnosed disease, and two attempts to treat it

Across every diagnostic the failure looked the same: the model was too confident. Bins it priced at 27% happened 12% of the time; bins it priced at 86% happened 25% of the time. This family attacked the width and shape of the distribution directly.

D1 · Student-t tails and the per-lead sigma floor

in service Kept — the honest-width guarantee

Replace the normal distribution with a heavier-tailed Student-t, and impose a hard minimum on the spread at each lead time.

Mechanism

The tail-heaviness parameter is estimated from how often reality actually lands far from the forecast. The sigma floor is the measured error at that lead on held-out data, and — uniquely in the whole system — it is exempt from self-gating. Everything else must prove it helps or be switched off; the floor may not be switched off, because it is the direct remedy for the failure that killed EMOS v1.

Result

Retained. It prevents the catastrophic overconfidence of v1, without being sufficient to produce an edge.

D2 · Widen the distribution when models disagree

tested 2026-07-11 Rejected — improved Brier, not ROI

When the model and the market disagree sharply, treat that disagreement as evidence of our own uncertainty and widen the predictive distribution accordingly.

Rationale

A large disagreement with a well-informed market is more likely to mean we have missed something than that we have found something. Widening in those cases should reduce confident errors.

Result

Brier improved. ROI did not. The pre-registered test required both, so it was rejected.

Taught us

The diagnosis was subtly wrong. Widening helps when a forecast is right but overconfident. It cannot help when the forecast is centred in the wrong place — a wider distribution around a wrong mean still bets on the wrong bin, just less aggressively. The problem was the centre, not the spread.

Family E

Combination and selectivity — bet less, bet only where you're strong

If the model cannot be made better everywhere, perhaps it can be trusted selectively — blended toward the price, or restricted to the situations where it historically did well. This family contains the project's most instructive self-inflicted wound.

E1 · Maximum-selection between predictors

removed Manufactured edge out of nothing

Where two predictors disagreed, the engine took the higher of the two probabilities.

Why it's fatal

Taking the maximum of two noisy estimates is not a forecast — it is a systematic upward bias. Whichever predictor happened to be more optimistic about a bin won, every time. The engine's "edge" was partly just this operator, and it grew with the noise in the inputs.

Fix

Replaced with a plain average, and measured edge dropped accordingly. That drop was the point.

Taught us

A one-word choice in a combination rule — max instead of mean — can invent an edge that survives every downstream check, because it corrupts the forecast before any evaluation sees it.

E2 · Shrink toward the market

available Damage limitation, not edge

Serve a weighted blend of our probability and the market price: w × model + (1−w) × market.

Mechanism

With w = 1 you bet the pure model. As w falls you deviate from the price only where the model disagrees strongly, and every position is sized down in proportion.

Result

Because the market out-predicts the model, the Brier-minimising weight is below 1 — the optimiser's advice is, in effect, be more like the price. It reduces losses. It cannot create an edge, because a blend of a worse forecast and a better one is bounded by the better one.

E3 · Per-bucket selectivity

rebuilt 2026-07-28 The pockets were noise

Split every market into a bucket by city and horizon — Seoul|1d, Chicago|same-day, fifteen in all — and bet only the buckets where the model historically beat the price.

Rationale

Pooled performance can hide real local skill. Perhaps the model is genuinely good at Seoul next-day and hopeless at New York same-day, in which case selective betting turns a losing system into a winning one.

What went wrong

Two buckets were nominated by eyeballing in-sample numbers. Then confidence intervals were added, and not one of the fifteen buckets had an interval excluding zero in the model's favour — while four had intervals entirely on the wrong side.

The decisive test was a simulation. Take the real data, remove all edge — force the model to be exactly as good as the market in every bucket — and ask what "pick the best bucket" produces:

And ranking by score systematically crowns the smallest bucket, because small samples generate the most extreme numbers in both directions — the five-market bucket wins a third of the time, the forty-eight-market bucket almost never.

Rebuilt as

All fifteen buckets are now nominated on identical prospective terms, each with its own forward clock, and the pass threshold is corrected for the fact that fifteen things are being tested at once. No human picks favourites; nothing is graded on the data that suggested it.

Family F

Structure books — trading the market's shape, with no forecast at all

A different theory entirely. Forget predicting the weather: look for systematic mispricing in how the market prices its bins. Betting markets across many domains display favourite–longshot bias — crowds overpay for unlikely outcomes and underpay for likely ones. If temperature bins show it too, you can harvest it without any meteorology.

F1 · Shoulder sell

since 2026-07-12 Unproven — interval spans zero

Sell bins priced between 5¢ and 35¢ the day before — the plausible-looking longshots two or three degrees off the forecast centre.

Result

159 settled contracts, +0.036 per contract, 87% win rate. And on 27 July it formally passed its pre-registered gate — the first thing in this project ever to do so.

It should not have counted, for two independent reasons.

Why it doesn't count

Underpowered. Over 54 independent city-days the interval was [−0.023, +0.070] — zero comfortably inside. The gate was a point estimate with no power requirement, and per-bet dispersion here is enormous relative to the edge: detecting a true 2¢ advantage at conventional power needs roughly 1,760 independent bets.

It doesn't replicate. The breadth book (below) runs the same strategy across 49 cities and puts the identical band at +0.005 — and flips the sign of the sub-band that looked strongest. Two samples disagreeing about which part carries the effect is what noise looks like.

The 87% win rate is worth understanding, because it is seductive and meaningless on its own: wins average +0.157, losses average −0.800. One loss cancels five wins. A strategy like this shows ~86% wins whether it is profitable or quietly bleeding — only the average distinguishes them.

F2 · Favourite buy

since 2026-07-13 Barely any sample

The mirror trade: buy YES on bins priced 65–85¢, the "boring" favourites the same bias should leave underpriced.

Result

One graded contract, which lost. Nothing can be concluded. Entries are restricted to more than 12 hours before the local day ends, and that window rarely coincides with prices in the target band.

F3 · Breadth book — all 49 cities

since 2026-07-23 The powered replication — reads zero

The same model-free legs extended from 5 cities to every active Polymarket weather market, graded off Polymarket's own settlements.

Why it exists

The 5-city book accrues sample too slowly to settle anything, and needs an independent check. Because these trades require no forecast, extending them costs nothing but code — no per-city weather plumbing, no resolution anchors. It grades directly off how each market paid out.

Result

947 settled contracts across 49 cities. Full band +0.005 per contract, interval tight around zero. Decomposed by price band, the picture contradicts the 5-city story outright: the band that looked strongest there (25–35¢) is negative here, and bins priced near 30¢ realise 30.2% — fairly priced, so selling them merely donates the spread and fee.

The one real finding

Splitting by liquidity produces the only statistically significant result in the entire structure programme, and it is a warning rather than an opportunity: in thin books (under $1k liquidity), resting maker orders lose −0.064 per contract, interval [−0.124, −0.005]. That is adverse selection made visible — your resting sell order fills precisely when the price is moving against you. Don't make markets where nobody trades.

Chronology

Nine times the measuring instrument was broken

This is the part of the project with the most transferable value. Every one of these was found after it had already produced numbers that were believed. Every single correction moved the result in the same direction — the model looked worse, the market looked better. Order matters here, because each fix changed the numbers the next stage was reasoning about.

  1. June 2026

    Grading bets against our own forecast grid

    Outcomes were graded using the same Open-Meteo grid the model forecast from. Prediction and "truth" therefore shared the same errors, so the model was rewarded for being consistently wrong. This produced the project's famous 127.5% ROI — entirely fictional. Grading against real station observations roughly halved measured returns.

  2. 3 July 2026

    The truth feed itself was corrupt

    Meteostat, the historical weather source, was found to be up to 9°C away from official readings on recent dates. Every number computed against it was meaningless. Replaced with National Weather Service climate reports, METAR summaries, and the Hong Kong Observatory API.

  3. July 2026

    Roughly 25 code bugs, one of them enormous

    The largest silently excluded 83% of US markets from grading. Others leaked future data into the backtest. Each was individually plausible; collectively they made every prior result unreliable.

  4. 12 July 2026

    We were reading the wrong thermometer

    These markets do not settle on the National Weather Service climate report. They settle on wunderground.com, whose daily extremes are computed from hourly observations rather than continuous sensors — a difference that can be exactly 1°F at a bin boundary. Four of sixty audited markets had been graded backwards. This destroyed the project's most promising result: an apparent New York same-day edge that was, on corrected labels, the mislabelled boundary days.

  5. 20 July 2026

    The correct thermometer, silently unused

    The settlement-faithful grader needs an hourly observations file. When that file is missing it returns nothing and the system falls back to the old source — without warning. The file was gitignored and written only by one job, so three of four cloud workflows had never once graded with it present. Where both sources existed they disagreed on 99 of 174 days, flipping 18 verdicts.

  6. 27 July 2026

    A gate that passed on a point estimate

    The shoulder book cleared its pre-registered threshold. The threshold had no power requirement, so "gate met" and "no edge whatsoever" were statistically indistinguishable. Every gate in the project now additionally requires its confidence interval to exclude zero across enough independent city-days.

  7. 27 July 2026

    A fill detector that could barely see

    Maker-order profitability was measured against a price history containing a median of one later observation per entry — 36% of entries had none at all and could never register a fill. The apparent maker edge was an artifact of near-blindness. Rebuilt on the dense price feed, fills rose from 34% to 55% and the apparent edge shrank.

  8. 28 July 2026

    A scoreboard comparing different markets

    The published dashboard scored the model over 401 markets and the ensemble over the 246 they shared, then captioned it "on the same markets". On genuinely equal footing the model was 0.148, not 0.1369 — losing, not winning.

  9. 28 July 2026

    A comparison frozen for days

    The daily job rebuilt only one of the two files the headline comparison needs. The other came from a manual job last run three days earlier, so the published verdict sat perfectly still while looking perfectly healthy.

The pattern worth taking away

Six of these nine were silent: no error, no failed job, no red run. A stale file, a missing column, an unmounted element, a swallowed exception. The system reported success while publishing something wrong.

The defence that now exists is not more tests but louder failure — builds that refuse to publish when a city is ungradable or an exception was caught, rather than shipping a partial page with a green tick.

Comparing the two forecasters on each individual market rather than averaging them separately cut the uncertainty by more than half — because most variation between markets is shared difficulty. "Will London hit 24°C tomorrow?" is genuinely uncertain and both forecasters score badly; comparing on the same question cancels that out and leaves only who was closer. Clustering then counts a city-day once rather than eleven times, since all eleven bins settle on a single temperature reading.

Current status

The forecasting book is off and has never traded real money. The structure books run on paper across 49 cities. All fifteen forecasting buckets are under prospective test with corrected thresholds; none has passed. No real capital has been deployed at any point in this project.

Synthesis

Six things four months bought

1 · The market is not a naive counterparty

The persistent assumption behind every model here was that crowd prices are sloppy and a proper physics-based forecast should beat them. On 261 markets the price beat every model, every baseline, and every combination — in all five cities. Its prices are best calibrated precisely where we disagreed with them most.

2 · Better on one metric is not better on the metric that pays

QRF v2 forecasts temperatures genuinely better than the ensemble in three of five cities, and still prices bins worse. Degrees and probabilities are different currencies, and the exchange rate is not in your favour when the question is which one-degree bucket a reading lands in.

3 · Public information is not an edge

Intraday conditioning was the best idea in the project, and the market prices the running maximum at least as well as we do. Being able to read a feed everyone else can read is not an advantage — it is table stakes, and you are competing on speed.

4 · Splitting data to find pockets mostly finds noise

Fifteen buckets, ranked by score, systematically crowns the smallest one. In a simulation with no edge at all, the apparent best bucket looks better than our real best bucket. If you must slice, pre-register every slice and correct for how many you looked at.

5 · Every measurement error flattered us

Nine broken instruments, nine corrections, all in the same direction. That is not coincidence — a bug that makes results look worse gets investigated immediately, while one that makes them look better gets celebrated. Assume the next correction will also cost you.

6 · Silent failure is the dominant bug class

The expensive errors here were not crashes. They were a stale file, a swallowed exception, an unmounted element, a comparison across mismatched samples — all reporting success. Systems that compute numbers need to fail loudly when an input is missing, because a plausible wrong number is far more dangerous than an obvious blank.

The honest bottom line

Twelve modelling approaches, four data sources, three distributional families, two book designs. The forecasting thread is closed: the model is measurably worse than the market, and the pockets where it looked better do not survive contact with a confidence interval.

What remains is the model-free structure programme, accruing sample across 49 cities and so far reading zero — with one significant finding, and it is a warning: don't provide liquidity where none is wanted.

Reference

Glossary

Bin
One temperature bucket in a market — "24°C" — settling YES or NO. About eleven per city-day, mutually exclusive.
Brier score
Mean squared error of a probability forecast. Lower is better; 0 is perfect. The project's scoreboard.
CRPS
Continuous Ranked Probability Score — the equivalent for a whole temperature distribution, measured in degrees.
Ensemble
Many runs of a weather model from slightly different starting conditions. Their spread is a physically meaningful uncertainty estimate.
EMOS
Ensemble Model Output Statistics — the standard statistical method for correcting an ensemble's bias and spread against observations.
Lead time
How far ahead a forecast is made. A 1-day forecast and a 5-day forecast are different problems and are modelled separately here.
METAR
The routine hourly weather report issued by airports. The raw material for both settlement and intraday conditioning.
Clustering (city-day)
Treating all bins for one city on one date as a single independent observation, because they all settle on one temperature reading.
Pre-registered gate
A success threshold committed to in writing before seeing the data, so it cannot be moved afterwards to fit a result.
Forward bet
A market that settled after a hypothesis was written down. The only kind that counts toward a gate.
Maker / taker
A maker posts a resting order and waits; a taker crosses the spread for immediate execution. Makers pay lower fees but get filled selectively — often precisely when the price is moving against them.
Favourite–longshot bias
The tendency across betting markets for unlikely outcomes to be overpriced and likely ones underpriced. The premise of the structure books.
